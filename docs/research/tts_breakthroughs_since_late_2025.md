# Keep Matcha's Durations, Engineer Pace Explicitly

**No system released between November 2025 and September 2026 beats your plan's core for zero-hallucination narration.** A Matcha-style model with Monotonic Alignment Search (MAS) and explicit per-phoneme durations is still the most defensible backbone, and the year's evidence against the alternatives made that case stronger. OmniVoice's single frame budget fell to **24% text coverage at 191 words** without chunking ([vllm-omni #6333](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm-omni/issues/6333)), and Qwen3-TTS-0.6B reached **35.2% worst-of-N WER** on prompts over 1,500 words ([Argmax](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.16989)). Chatterbox Multilingual v3, the only autoregressive model that publishes a Czech figure, measured **32.21% Czech CER** ([Resemble AI](https://www.resemble.ai/resources/chatterbox-multilingual-v3-tts-with-embedded-watermarking-for-25-languages)). The breakthroughs worth taking are add-ons rather than replacements: reinforcement learning that now works on flow-matching decoders and duration predictors, contrastive negatives that simulate repeated and skipped words, self-purifying data filtering (a September 2025 method that posted the lowest WER in a December 2025 challenge), ASR judges that handle Czech and Slovak, 2,806 hours of new Slovak speech, and metrics built for long-form output. Key baseline assumptions also failed: WavLM-Large, the planned discriminator backbone, was pretrained on English only, and espeak-ng has had no Czech or Slovak rule changes since December 2024. ElevenLabs' pace problems fit models whose durations are implicit and sampled. Your system fixes pace by making tempo an explicit, fixed input to a deterministic duration predictor, keeping style vectors out of the duration path, rendering sentence by sentence under constant conditioning, flagging paragraph-final cadence instead of letting the model infer "end of input", and gating every render on measured articulation rate. Component by component: keep Matcha/MAS/OT-CFM on mel, BigVGAN v2, MPD/MRD and your normalizer; replace the WavLM backbone and the ECAPA conditioning; and add rate conditioning, robustness objectives, RL post-training, a G2P correction layer, multilingual pretraining and ASR verification. Before any of that, fix seven defects in the implementation plan. They include a length regulator that deletes short phonemes, a flow-matching sampler that integrates in the wrong direction, and a phoneme vocabulary that maps 41–62% of espeak-ng's real Czech and Slovak tokens to `<unk>`. Left in place, they would cause exactly the failures this architecture was chosen to prevent.

## No 2026 release beats explicit durations for zero-skip narration

### Autoregressive models improved on short text, not on long text

Most of the window's releases were autoregressive (AR) and LLM-based TTS: roughly twenty open-weight AR or hybrid models, from Qwen3-TTS in January to Breeze TTS 2 in August. **RL post-training with ASR rewards became standard.** Fish Audio S2 combines ASR, acoustic-preference and speaker-similarity rewards aimed explicitly at "hallucinations, token skipping, and timbre drift" ([Fish S2 report](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.08823v2)). Qwen3-TTS runs DPO followed by GSPO ([Qwen3-TTS report](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15621v1)). NVIDIA's Magpie trains with monotonic attention priors, CFG and GRPO ([Magpie card](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/magpie_tts_multilingual_357m)).

Short-form error fell to about 1% (Fish S2: 0.99% English WER), but **long-form error stayed several times higher**. Fish S2 itself reports 4.38% English long-form WER. Argmax's September study measured Qwen3-TTS-1.7B at 29.9% mean and 47.8% worst-of-N WER on inputs of 1,500 words or more ([Argmax](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.16989)). Argmax's inference-only LACI patch monitors attention heads, rolls back, and imposes temporary hard alignment masks. It brought those figures down to 2.8% and 4.2%, and cut the 0.6B model's worst-of-N WER from 35.2% to 3.4%. That the repair for AR long-form failure is temporary hard alignment is telling, because MAS imposes that constraint permanently.

Practitioners reached the same conclusion. The most widely used open audiobook tool still generates, transcribes with Whisper, retries, and "cherrypick[s] the generation with the least number of errors" ([tts-audiobook-tool](https://github.com/zeropointnine/tts-audiobook-tool)). It has added detectors for Fish's dropped end phrases, VibeVoice's "music hallucinations" and Chatterbox's end-of-prompt noises. Its transcript validation is first-class only in English and Spanish.

Czech and Slovak make the AR picture worse. Only six AR releases claim either language:

| Model | Czech | Slovak | Weights |
|---|---|---|---|
| Higgs Audio v3 | Yes | Yes | Non-commercial |
| Fish S2 Pro | Yes | Yes | Non-commercial |
| KugelAudio-0-open | Yes | Yes | MIT, 7B |
| MOSS-TTS v1.5 | Yes | No | Apache-2.0 |
| Chatterbox Multilingual v3 | Yes | No | MIT |
| ZONOS2 | No | Yes | Apache-2.0 |

**None of them publishes a long-form Czech or Slovak error rate.** Higgs v3 places both languages in a "Tier 1" defined as WER/CER below 5% ([Higgs v3](https://huggingface.co/bosonai/higgs-audio-v3-tts-4b)), far from audiobook grade. Chatterbox v3 measured Czech at 32.21% CER, against under 0.20% for Italian and German ([Resemble AI](https://www.resemble.ai/resources/chatterbox-multilingual-v3-tts-with-embedded-watermarking-for-25-languages)). A community Slovak Chatterbox fine-tune warns of "quiet, garbled audio mid-utterance on hard inputs" ([pekiskol](https://huggingface.co/pekiskol/chatterbox-tts-slovak)). No systematic comparison of AR and non-autoregressive (NAR) hallucination rates appeared. DELTA-TTS converts an AR model into a diffusion language model to reduce hallucinations, but compares only against its own backbone ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.04140)).

### New non-autoregressive models failed where durations were implicit

The two notable new NAR models that cover both languages traded explicit alignment for scale.

**OmniVoice** (Xiaomi/k2-fsa, April 2026) is a masked discrete-diffusion model over codec tokens for 646 languages. It reports 2.856% Czech WER on the MiniMax multilingual set, where ElevenLabs scored 2.108, and it ships fine-tuning code ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.00688v2)). A third-party re-run in Supertonic's README measured OmniVoice at 2.40 on the same Czech set ([Supertonic](https://github.com/supertone-inc/supertonic)). Its data, however, holds only **148 h of Czech against 2,478 h of Slovak** ([languages.md](https://github.com/k2-fsa/OmniVoice/blob/master/docs/languages.md)). Because it "commits one frame budget for the entire input", its text coverage without chunking fell from clean at 93 words to 89.8–96.6% at 118 words, 24% at 191 words and 0% at 309 words, while the server still returned HTTP 200 ([vllm-omni #6333](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm-omni/issues/6333)). Chunking at about 15 s, which upstream OmniVoice does by default, raised coverage to 96.4–97.5% in the same test. But chunking brings chunk-to-chunk drift "in timbre or even speed" ([#44](https://github.com/k2-fsa/OmniVoice/issues/44)). Users also report Polish sentence endings cut off ([#245](https://github.com/k2-fsa/OmniVoice/issues/245)), and "random long pauses in the middle of sentences" after a 3 h Romanian audiobook fine-tune ([#19](https://github.com/k2-fsa/OmniVoice/issues/19)).

**Supertonic 3** (Supertone, released April 29, 2026) covers Czech and Slovak with about 99M parameters. It is a flow-matching text-to-latent model whose official inference defaults to 8 denoising steps. It shipped only preset voices and no training code. A July 23, 2026 notice then archived the project: the weights moved to supertone-oss-archive/supertonic-3, and the Voice Builder service for custom voices closed on August 31, 2026 ([GitHub](https://github.com/supertone-inc/supertonic); [HF](https://huggingface.co/Supertone/supertonic-3)).

These failures are structural. FreyaTTS's reviewers note that "single global duration prediction cannot prevent word-skip and long-horizon drift" ([Pith](https://pith.science/paper/2607.09530)). Supertone built a dedicated anti-repeat, anti-skip training objective for its own compact model ([RobustSpeechFlow](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.22083v1)), and F5-TTS scored 9.00% CER on the Seed hard set before RL fine-tuning, and still 7.86% after it ([FlowTTS-GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.23190)). PFluxTTS shows the same thing from the other side: fusing a duration-guided vector field into an alignment-free one cut CER from 14.1% to 8.6% ([PFluxTTS](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.04160v1)). A model that holds one frame budget for a whole input compresses, drops or truncates content whenever that estimate is wrong. One caveat stands: **no in-window study compares explicit and implicit durations under matched data**. The absence of failure reports for Matcha-style models also partly reflects how few people scrutinize them.

### The explicit-duration lineage went dormant upstream but still wins listeners

Matcha-TTS's last PyPI release is still from November 2024 ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/matcha-tts/#history)). Matcha co-author Gustav Eje Henter appears on VoXtream2, a streaming model whose speaking rate can be changed mid-utterance ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13518)), but no Matcha successor exists. hexgrad has published nothing since Kokoro-82M in April 2025 ([HF](https://huggingface.co/hexgrad)).

Even so, **Kokoro-82M ranks #6 on Artificial Analysis's open-weights arena (Elo 1063), far above StyleTTS 2 at #15 (892)** ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/text-to-speech/leaderboard/provider-voice/open-weights)). Kokoro is a small explicit-duration model with StyleTTS2-style adversarial training. Its ranking is the best real-world evidence that the combination your plan uses can still win on human preference.

The lineage reached West Slavic languages through community work. The kokoro-pl project mapped espeak-ng's Polish output into Kokoro's phoneme vocabulary and trained a base stage on 20 h from 20 narrators. It then fine-tuned on about 4 h per narrator, at roughly $3–4 for 10 epochs on an A10 ([kokoro-pl](https://github.com/gicaking/kokoro-pl)).

At the commercial ceiling, Cartesia Sonic 3.6 leads the Speech Arena at 1279, ahead of Gemini 3.8 Flash TTS (1265) and Eleven v3 (1169) ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/text-to-speech/leaderboard)). Gemini 3.8 Flash TTS became generally available on September 22 ([Gemini changelog](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/changelog)). **Only Sonic 3.6 and MiniMax Speech 2.8 document both Czech and Slovak** ([Cartesia](https://docs.cartesia.ai/build-with-cartesia/tts-models/latest); [MiniMax](https://platform.minimax.io/docs/api-reference/speech-t2a-http)); MiniMax does so through a language-boost list shared across its speech models. No arena measures either language. ElevenLabs output also cannot be used in your training set: its policy prohibits using output "as input for any machine learning or training of artificial intelligence models" ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/use-policy)).

The developments that matter arrived as components rather than whole systems. They are RL for decoders and duration predictors, contrastive robustness objectives, multilingual self-supervised (SSL) speech encoders, Czech and Slovak ASR, Slovak corpora and long-form metrics. ## Two replacements and nine additions leave the core intact

The confidence levels in the table below mean the following. **High** rests on direct in-window or primary-source evidence plus a clear mechanism. **Medium** rests on evidence from other languages or architectures, or on a strong mechanism without a direct test. **Low** marks a plausible inference that nobody has tested.

One fact organizes every verdict. In a Matcha-style model, **MAS and the duration predictor fix content fidelity, the duration predictor alone fixes tempo, and the decoder and vocoder determine timbre and audio quality**. Each upgrade can therefore target exactly one layer.

### The text front end is now the likeliest source of Czech and Slovak errors

Once alignment is hard and durations are explicit, most word errors a listener hears will come from text processing, and here the ecosystem did not move. **Your declension-aware normalizer is ahead of every open tool.** NVIDIA's NeMo-text-processing still has no Czech or Slovak grammars. OmniVoice falls back to num2words "for integers" ([OmniVoice](https://github.com/k2-fsa/OmniVoice)). The Slovak Chatterbox model card tells users to rewrite "20 %" as "dvadsať percent" by hand ([pekiskol](https://huggingface.co/pekiskol/chatterbox-tts-slovak)).

The one useful new check is Unicode CLDR's spell-out rules. They carry cardinal and ordinal variants for every gender and case, including the Czech masculine-animate accusative and the Slovak masculine-human plural ordinals ([cs.xml](https://github.com/unicode-org/cldr/blob/main/common/rbnf/cs.xml); [sk.xml](https://github.com/unicode-org/cldr/blob/main/common/rbnf/sk.xml)). Generating the full number × case × gender grid through ICU and diffing it against your code is a cheap regression test.

What remains to build is context handling. That covers abbreviations, units that agree with their numbers, dates, Roman numerals and, hardest of all, choosing the case from the governing word, which needs a morphological tagger. If an LLM ever helps, treat its output as a proposal that rules must verify. Granary used this pattern, with a guard that rejects edits above 5% CER ([Granary](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.13404)).

G2P is where the baseline was too optimistic. **espeak-ng's latest release is still 1.52 from December 2024. In about 300 commits since November 2025, the only Czech or Slovak file changes were emoji data** ([releases](https://github.com/espeak-ng/espeak-ng/releases)). A documented Czech voicing error has no Czech-specific fix: "rozhlas" is phonemized as [roshlas], because a voicing switch fires after transcription ([#1951](https://github.com/orgs/espeak-ng/discussions/1951)).

Keep espeak-ng as the engine, but add a correction layer around it. Put a word-level exception lexicon in front of it, and post-rules for voicing assimilation across word and preposition boundaries after it. For Slovak, add checks for the rhythmic law and for ä, ô and ľ. The strongest replacement candidate is a rule-based Czech/Slovak/Polish IPA converter built from the phonetics literature ([lukyjanek](https://github.com/lukyjanek/phonetic-transcription)). Its licence and accuracy were not captured, so trial it against your lexicon rather than switching blind. The alternatives are thin. Epitran has a Czech map but none for Slovak ([Epitran](https://github.com/dmort27/epitran)), and MFA publishes Czech models but nothing for Slovak ([MFA](https://mfa-models.readthedocs.io/en/latest/acoustic/index.html)).

English names inside Slovak or Czech text need their own path. Add a per-token language embedding in the encoder, the approach IndexTTS 2.5 uses to add languages ([IndexTTS 2.5](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2601.03888)), and phonemize tagged spans as English. GLM-TTS's "hybrid phoneme-text input scheme" for pronunciation control ([GLM-TTS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.14291)) suggests a further step: train with random phoneme substitution of word spans, so the model accepts inline overrides. That transfer to Matcha is untested.

Hold off on a Czech substitute for PL-BERT. The one directly relevant study, "Evaluating Phoneme-Level Pretraining in Czech Text-to-Speech Synthesis" (TSD 2025), could not be read ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-02548-7_14)).

### Robustness objectives and RL slot into the decoder without touching alignment

The cheapest decoder win is **RobustSpeechFlow's contrastive loss**, ℒ = ℒ_pos − 0.2·ℒ_rand − 0.2·ℒ_aug ([RobustSpeechFlow](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.22083v1)). Its augmented negatives are deliberate failure-mode corruptions of the target. A "repeat" overwrites 20–40% of the target with content from another span. A "skip" shifts the sequence forward and pads it with silence. Both preserve length, so they fit MAS-expanded targets directly. On SupertonicTTS, the loss cut ZERO500 CER from 0.48% to 0.35% in English and from 0.81% to 0.57% in Korean. Gains were larger at fewer sampling steps, and the method needs no aligner or preference data. No code was released.

**Self-Purifying Flow Matching (SPFM)** compares each sample's conditional and unconditional loss, and routes suspicious text–audio pairs to unconditional training. The method dates from September 2025, just before the window ([alphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2509.19091)). The in-window result is Supertone's WildSpoof 2026 entry, Supertonic fine-tuned with SPFM, which posted the lowest WER of all teams in the TTS track ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.17293)). Audiobook recordings in which the narrator departs from the text are exactly those suspicious pairs. SPFM needs a text-dropout unconditional branch, which vanilla Matcha lacks; adding one also enables classifier-free guidance.

**RL for flow matching became usable in the window, but its gains land on quality more than on content.** FlowTTS-GRPO turns early ODE steps into SDE windows and trains a rank-32 LoRA on speaker-similarity, 1−WER and DNSMOS rewards weighted 1.0/1.0/0.4. In its CosyVoice 3 setup that LoRA is 10.09M parameters, 2.78% of the flow-matching model ([FlowTTS-GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.23190)). On F5-TTS it moved English WER from 1.88% to 1.73% and similarity from 0.753 to 0.790, in only 1,289 steps on 8 GPUs. On CosyVoice 3's flow-matching stage, however, similarity rose from 0.830 to 0.859 while CER slipped from 1.20 to 1.26. The authors conclude that intelligibility RL suits language models and audio-detail RL suits flow matching. GROW is simpler to bolt on ([GROW](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.03215v1)). It weights the ordinary flow-matching regression loss by group-relative advantages, anchors the velocity field to a frozen copy, and needs no SDE conversion. It cut WER from 2.016% to 1.558% on its base model, and its 10-step rollouts matched 32-step ones at 2.9× the speed. Its code is only promised.

For your model, decoder RL buys timbre and quality. RL on the duration predictor is what could reduce the mumbled, too-short phonemes that ASR counts as deletions. That is the DMOSpeech 2 pattern, published at AAAI 2026 ([DMOSpeech 2](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/40450)). Its repository has inference code and checkpoints, but the training code is still marked "under construction" ([GitHub](https://github.com/yl4579/DMOSpeech2)). Aalto's EmphTTS (September 23, 2026) reports that GRPO on a NAR duration predictor beat supervised duration modelling for emphasis, but its numbers could not be read ([alphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.27599)).

The weak point is the reward. The CER term needs a strong Czech/Slovak ASR, and MOS predictors are English-centric, so they belong at low weight. A similarity reward that pulls toward one narrator centroid risks collapsing prosodic variety, so keep the velocity anchor and listening tests in the loop. None of these methods has been tested on a MAS-based model or on any Slavic language.

Three tempting changes lack support. **Moving from mel to VAE latents** pays off only with semantic regularization: in F5-TTS a vanilla VAE raised WER from 2.23% (mel) to 2.65%, while Semantic-VAE reached 2.10% and SARA reached 1.79% against a 2.42% F5-TTS baseline, both in English only ([Semantic-VAE](https://awesomepapers.io/speech-audio/papers/2509.22167); [SARA](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.11611)). The popular latents are also too coarse for per-phoneme durations: VoxCPM2's 6.25 Hz tokens each span 160 ms, longer than many phonemes, and dots.tts runs at 25 Hz. Mel at 24 kHz with hop 256 gives roughly 94 frames per second.

**Replacing the U-Net with a DiT** also lacks support at your scale. Every new NAR system uses one, but each trained on hundreds to hundreds of thousands of hours, and nothing shows a DiT beating Matcha's U-Net on 10–40 h. Revisit it only after multilingual pretraining. **Few-step distillation**, such as DSFlow or X-Pred MeanFlow, matters little for offline rendering on H200/B200 and risks flatter prosody across a chapter.

### WavLM and ECAPA rest on assumptions the research overturned

**The SLM discriminator's backbone is English-only.** The November baseline justified WavLM-Large as working "for ANY language". The WavLM paper says otherwise: its 94k hours are Libri-Light, GigaSpeech and English VoxPopuli, chosen because "our focus is English-only audio" ([WavLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2110.13900v5)). SSL models "typically degrade on unfamiliar languages" ([codec generalization study](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.12205)). That is why TTSDS2's multilingual variant swaps its English SSL features for mHuBERT-147 and XLSR-53 ([TTSDS2 notes](https://en.papernotes.org/ICLR2026/audio_speech/ttsds2_resources_and_benchmark_for_evaluating_human-quality_text_to_speech_syste/)).

The candidate replacements compare as follows.

No paper has yet swapped WavLM for a multilingual encoder in an SLM discriminator. So run the ablation: no SLM discriminator, WavLM, XLS-R or Omnilingual, and w2v-BERT 2.0. Score each by Czech/Slovak CER and a small native-listener panel. TTSDS2's multilingual mode can add only a secondary, uncalibrated signal, because none of its 14 languages is Czech or Slovak.

When porting StyleTTS2's discriminator code, audit the loss wiring. Among five StyleTTS2 training bugs, the Kikiri-TTS team found that "The ground-truth computation for WavLM adversarial loss was accidentally deleted" ([Kikiri-TTS](https://github.com/semidark/kikiri-tts/discussions/8)). They also found GAN gating keyed to the wrong epoch variable, and a disabled diffusion sampler feeding garbage to the discriminator. Because the SLM path runs mel → BigVGAN → SSL encoder, fine-tune and freeze the vocoder first. Keep the adversarial phase short and late, to avoid overfitting 10–40 h of data.

If adversarial training proves unstable, there are two fallbacks. A discriminator on frozen-vocoder features matched waveform discriminators in voice-conversion distillation, with 9.6× less training time and 11.4× less memory ([VPFD](https://pith.science/paper/2508.17874)). FlowTTS-GRPO's DNSMOS reward offers an RL alternative to adversarial training.

**ECAPA conditioning adds drift without adding a voice.** For one narrator, per-utterance ECAPA vectors mostly encode channel, session and prosody. One team's cross-lingual speaker-verification system for the TidyVoice 2026 challenge, LI-MSV, added gradient-reversal training specifically to make its embeddings "language-invariant" ([LI-MSV](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08092)). That fix is needed only if ordinary embeddings carry language. Vectors from your Czech, Slovak and English sessions can therefore carry language too, which FiLM/AdaIN conditioning would entangle with voice. At inference you would also have to pick a reference vector, another source of drift. Replace the conditioning with one learned narrator embedding, or a learned speaker table during multi-speaker pretraining. Move speaker encoders to evaluation, where ReDimNet2-B6 (MIT, 0.29% VoxCeleb1-O EER) and a w2v-BERT 2.0 verifier (0.12%) far outperform SpeechBrain ECAPA (0.80%) ([ReDimNet2](https://github.com/PalabraAI/redimnet2); [w2v-BERT SV](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2510.04213); [ECAPA](https://huggingface.co/speechbrain/spkrec-ecapa-voxceleb)).

Two rules apply when evaluating similarity. Compare it only within a language and with one extractor: the same SupertonicTTS system scores 0.472 with WavLM-TDNN and 0.915 with WavLM Base+ ([SupertonicTTS](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.23108v2)). And pair it with rhythm metrics, because verification embeddings "focus mainly on static features like timbre and pitch range, while neglecting dynamic elements such as rhythm" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02176)).

**The vocoder needs no change.** No NVIDIA successor to BigVGAN v2 appeared, and its 24 kHz checkpoint expects 100 Slaney-normalized mel bands ([BigVGAN](https://github.com/NVIDIA/BigVGAN); [config](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/bigvgan_v2_24khz_100band_256x/raw/main/config.json)). Vocoders do not cause skipped or repeated words. Fine-tuning BigVGAN on the narrator and on ground-truth-aligned predicted mels is therefore worth more than switching.

Flow2GAN (ICLR 2026, Apache-2.0) is the only in-window challenger with universal 24 kHz and 44 kHz checkpoints. It scores PESQ 4.203 at 4 steps against BigVGAN-v2's 3.945 on its authors' 24 kHz universal-audio (RFWave) test set ([Flow2GAN](https://github.com/k2-fsa/Flow2GAN)). Its 44 kHz 128-band 512x checkpoint is a cheap A/B if its mel settings match BigVGAN's, which no one has verified. That configuration keeps mel at about 86 frames per second, still ample for per-phoneme durations.

### Slovak finally has data, and both languages now have ASR judges

**The narrator's 10–40 h is enough to train the voice.** BanglaKontho's 20 h single-narrator audiobook corpus trained an MB-iSTFT-VITS from scratch to 9.5% WER and MOS 4.46. The same architecture trained on a general corpus reached only 16.0% and 3.16 ([BanglaKontho](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.29146)). Pretraining is for robustness on rare words, names and numbers, and for Slovak it is now possible.

If you add synthetic speech, follow ZeSTA: a lightweight real-versus-synthetic domain embedding, oversampled real data, and "real" selected at inference ([ZeSTA](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.04219)). Hold out the 2 h CC0 LibriVox Czech set as a read-literature test ([Mozilla Data Collective](https://mozilladatacollective.com/datasets/cmo0jfvnw00p1nx070preklt5)).

**Czech and Slovak now have ASR good enough to act as judges.** Use a transducer as the primary judge: Parakeet-TDT-0.6B-v3 covers both languages (FLEURS WER 11.01% Czech, 8.82% Slovak), with word and character timestamps, under CC-BY-4.0 ([Parakeet v3](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3)). Encoder-decoder models can invent text. Canary-1B-v2 scores 7.86% Czech WER, yet produced 134.7 hallucinated characters per minute on MUSAN noise in NVIDIA's own test ([Canary](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/canary-1b-v2)). Such models serve best as a second opinion from a different model family. Use Canary for Czech, the SloPal whisper-large-v3-sk fine-tune for Slovak at 5.5% FLEURS WER ([SloPal](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19270)), or Omnilingual's 7B LLM-ASR, which reaches CER 1.9 for Czech and 4.5 for Slovak ([Omnilingual CSV](https://github.com/facebookresearch/omnilingual-asr/blob/main/per_language_results_table_7B_llm_asr.csv)). Omnilingual's standard CTC and LLM models accept clips under 40 s. A December 2025 update added "Unlimited" LLM variants that take audio of any length ([Omnilingual](https://github.com/facebookresearch/omnilingual-asr)).

Human speech itself scores 5.5–11% WER on these systems, so **thresholds must be relative**. Run the same ASR on the narrator's real recordings of similar text and normalize both sides into one spoken form. Then flag disagreement between ASR families, contiguous deletions, repeated n-grams and over-long words; the last two checks come from Granary's hallucination filters. Every regression run should also report Argmax's skip count (10 or more contiguous deleted words) and hallucination count (20 or more contiguous inserted or substituted words), plus worst-of-N WER across seeds, catastrophic-failure rate, and windowed speaker similarity (wSIM) over 8 s windows at a 4 s stride ([Argmax](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.16989)).

One implication is specific to your architecture: **durations are deterministic, so a retry that only reseeds the decoder cannot fix a duration-caused skip**. A useful retry changes something that matters: a local length scale on the flagged word, a lexicon override, or a different sentence split.

For checkpoint selection, **TTSDS2 is the most robust suite, but it has not been validated for your languages** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.19441)). In its English listening data, its average Spearman correlation with human ratings is 0.67, and it was the only metric above 0.5 in all 12 domain-by-score combinations; UTMOS, DNSMOS, FAD and PESQ all fell below 0.3 ([TTSDS2 notes](https://en.papernotes.org/ICLR2026/audio_speech/ttsds2_resources_and_benchmark_for_evaluating_human-quality_text_to_speech_syste/)). Its multilingual benchmark covers 14 languages (ar, de, en, es, fr, it, ja, ko, nl, pl, pt, ru, tr and zh), none of them Czech or Slovak; Polish and Russian are the nearest ([leaderboard data](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ttsds/leaderboard_data)). Treat it as unvalidated for Czech and Slovak, and recalibrate it against native-listener ratings before letting it pick checkpoints. Its ICLR 2026 acceptance is confirmed only by a third-party listing, and the repository still cites the arXiv preprint ([ttsds](https://github.com/ttsds/ttsds)).

Use the other automatic judges only for relative, same-text comparisons. UTMOS's "validity for non-English languages … has not been formally established" ([T5Gemma-TTS](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.01760v1)). SpeechJudge-GRM agrees with humans only 77.2% of the time, even in English and Chinese ([SpeechJudge](https://huggingface.co/RMSnow/SpeechJudge-GRM)).

Automatic metrics also miss language-specific contrasts. A 2026 phonology-informed study found an Assamese system realizing mid [+ATR] vowels as [−ATR] in about a third of tokens, and argues that MOS and WER miss such errors ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.01965v1)). Add native minimal-pair checks for Czech vowel length, ř and consonant clusters.

One inexpensive hedge is worth running: a kokoro-pl-style Kokoro fine-tune for Czech or Slovak, as a benchmark for the Matcha build. Bear in mind that the Polish recipe does not say whether the StyleTTS2 aligner and PL-BERT were frozen, fine-tuned or bypassed, and publishes no WER or MOS.

## Pace drift is a duration problem, so make tempo an input

### ElevenLabs' symptoms match implicit, sampled durations

ElevenLabs attributes pacing mainly to training audio: "The pacing of the audio is highly influenced by the audio used to create the voice". It recommends "longer, continuous samples" to avoid pacing issues ([best practices](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/capabilities/text-to-speech/best-practices)). Its Professional Voice Clone (PVC) guide asks for "closer to 2-3 hours" of audio, against your 1.5 h. It warns that "the speaking style in the samples you provide will be replicated" and recommends a single style ([PVC guide](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-creative/voices/voice-cloning/professional-voice-cloning)).

Within a single request, the documentation names four risks. Style exaggeration can cause "inconsistent speed", so it should stay at 0. Long conversions can degrade, so text should be broken "into sections under 800 characters" ([troubleshooting](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-creative/troubleshooting)). Stability governs "the randomness between each generation", and seeded determinism "is not guaranteed" ([API](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/api-reference/text-to-speech/convert)).

**No ElevenLabs page acknowledges an end-of-generation slowdown.** The most consistent explanation, and it is a hypothesis, has three parts. Multilingual v2 conditions on the whole request, so it knows where the text ends. It learned a wind-down from PVC clips ending at paragraph or chapter boundaries, where final lengthening grows with boundary strength ([Wightman et al. 1992](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.402450), cited from prior knowledge). And it applies that wind-down at every request end. The API reference documents that `next_text` can "influence the speech's continuity in the current generation", which fits this reading ([API reference](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/api-reference/text-to-speech/convert)).

The "slow mode" on a different text has several compatible causes: sampling randomness between generations, narrative cues that ElevenLabs says steer pace, and a 1.5 h dataset in which a few slow sessions weigh heavily.

Open AR models show the same family of failures in measurable form. In Qwen3-TTS issue #239, a contributor measured **+16.7% rate drift within about 25 s** of streaming voice cloning with a 12.9 s reference clip ([#239](https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-TTS/issues/239)). A 4 s reference gave −2.3%, and non-streaming mode 0.0%. The rate drifted toward the model's prior of about 195 wpm whatever the reference's pace (112–182 wpm). Tempo in these models therefore hinges on conditioning details, such as reference length and streaming mode, rather than on the text. In issue #290, text that should have taken about a minute came out at 41–48 s, with the compression intensifying "toward the end" ([#290](https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-TTS/issues/290)). Chatterbox documents that higher `exaggeration` speeds speech up, while lower `cfg_weight` slows it ([README](https://github.com/resemble-ai/chatterbox)). The mechanisms follow from how these models work. Durations emerge token by token with no global budget, and the model conditions on its own output, so a slow patch reinforces itself. The reference prompt's influence fades over long contexts, the model sees where the text ends, and guidance knobs move tempo as a side effect.

That is why in-window systems are making duration explicit even inside AR decoders. IndexTTS2 has a token-count mode. MOSS-TTS has a `tokens=` budget and `[pause Xs]` markers ([MOSS-TTS](https://github.com/OpenMOSS/MOSS-TTS)). VoXtream2's speaking rate can be changed mid-utterance ([VoXtream2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13518)). The problem is widespread: averaged across the systems SwanBench-Speech tested, long-form prosodic coherence, which covers pauses and speaking rate, scored 3.43 against 4.04 for real speech ([SwanBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.28618)).

### Matcha decides tempo in one module, which is both the fix and the risk

Matcha's upstream source settles how its tempo controls work. It computes durations as `ceil(exp(logw)) * length_scale` and passes `temperature` only to the decoder ([matcha_tts.py](https://github.com/shivammehta25/Matcha-TTS/blob/main/matcha/models/matcha_tts.py)). Its CLI maps `--speaking_rate` to `length_scale` ("a higher value means slower speaking rate"), and describes `temperature` as the "Variance of the x0 noise" ([cli.py](https://github.com/shivammehta25/Matcha-TTS/blob/main/matcha/cli.py)).

**Tempo is therefore decided entirely by the duration predictor plus one scalar.** Durations are predicted in parallel from text and conditioning, and nothing conditions on generated audio. Accumulating drift, self-reinforcement and prompt fade therefore cannot occur.

Two mechanisms survive, and style diffusion would add a third. First, the transformer encoder reads the whole input, including where it ends. If training clips end in paragraph-final cadence, the duration predictor will learn "near the end, go slower", which is the ElevenLabs symptom in NAR form. Second, a deterministic predictor learns the average duration for each text, so session tempo it cannot explain becomes noise. That noise either flattens pace or attaches to text features correlated with slow sessions, such as reflective passages recorded on tired evenings. Third, a style vector in the duration path moves tempo directly: StyleTTS2's duration predictor consumes the style vector, so sampling a new style per sentence changes pace. That is why StyleTTS2's long-form recipe blends each sentence's style with the previous one at t = 0.7 ([StyleTTS2 notebook](https://github.com/yl4579/StyleTTS2/blob/main/Demo/Inference_LibriTTS.ipynb)).

### Eight rules turn tempo into a controlled input

These rules synthesize the evidence above; no in-window paper tests rate *consistency*, as opposed to rate control, in explicit-duration NAR models. The first four put tempo under explicit control. **Keep the duration predictor deterministic**, and verify in code that no noise reaches it. **Condition it on explicit rates**: give it a per-utterance articulation-rate input, meaning phones per second excluding pauses of 100–150 ms or longer, computed from MAS or MFA durations, and a separate pause-rate feature. This follows Data-Speech's `nb_phonemes / utterance_length` measure with long-silence removal and histogram bins ([dataspeech](https://github.com/huggingface/dataspeech)). At inference, fix both to the narrator's median or a per-book target for every sentence of every book, so the predictor can no longer pick a slow mode from text meaning or session noise. **Treat pauses as a separate channel**: keep punctuation as tokens so MAS attributes silences to boundaries, add paragraph and chapter tokens, and insert inter-sentence and paragraph pauses by rule from the narrator's measured medians. **Match inference segmentation to training segmentation**: cut training clips at sentence boundaries with identical rules across sessions and flag paragraph-final and chapter-final sentences. Then render sentence by sentence under fixed conditioning, and apply paragraph cadence only through the flag, never through "end of input".

The other four rules keep that control from leaking. **Keep style out of the duration path**, or use a fixed narration style or smoothing at t ≥ 0.7. **Use one fixed narrator embedding** rather than per-utterance ECAPA vectors that carry session tempo. **Calibrate one length scale per language**, since Slovak, Czech and English rates differ, and hold it constant within a book. And if you later tune durations with GRPO, **add a rate-consistency term** to the reward, covering distance from the target rate and the slope of rate against paragraph position. WER- and similarity-only rewards could drift toward slower, "safer" durations; that is an untested hypothesis. Sample durations around the deterministic mean only during rollouts, and deploy the mean.

The data needs the same discipline. Measure per-utterance articulation rate, speaking rate, pause durations by boundary type and paragraph position, then plot per-session and per-chapter medians. Flag utterances beyond about ±2 SD or ±10–15% of the global median; these thresholds are proposals. Keep expressive outliers with their rate labels, and drop rushed or disfluent takes. Trim every clip by one rule with fixed 100–200 ms pads, and balance sessions or add a session embedding fixed at inference. Optionally time-stretch a small subset by ±5–10%, with updated labels, to separate the rate axis from other factors. For alignment, MFA has pretrained Czech and English models but none for Slovak ([MFA](https://mfa-models.readthedocs.io/en/latest/acoustic/index.html)). For Slovak, use MAS durations from your own model, NeMo Forced Aligner with Parakeet-v3, Omnilingual CTC under its 40 s limit, or an MFA model trained on SloPalSpeech.

### Measure pace the way you measure word errors

The thresholds below are proposals anchored to the roughly 5% just-noticeable difference (JND) for tempo ([Quené 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.wocn.2006.09.001), not re-verified). No industry standard exists.

| Metric | What it measures | Proposed pass threshold |
|---|---|---|
| M1 Sentence articulation rate | Phones per second excluding pauses of 100 ms or more, on non-final syllables and overall, since final syllables lengthen naturally | Flag any sentence more than 10–12% from target |
| M2 End-of-text slowdown | Median rate of the last two sentences ÷ median of the preceding ones, plus the slope of rate against position | Ratio between 0.95 and 1.05 |
| M3 Position A/B | The same paragraph rendered as the final one vs followed by more text | Difference of 3–5% or less |
| M4 Cross-text consistency | Coefficient of variation of chapter-median rate, and of pause durations by boundary type | No higher than the narrator's own on held-out recordings; book median within ±5% of target |
| M5 Probe sentence | One fixed sentence embedded in many different texts | Identical under sentence-level rendering; within the JND once context conditioning is added |
| M6 Distribution match | Wasserstein distance between synthetic and human sentence-rate distributions, by paragraph position | Track; no standard exists |

Compute rates with an aligner independent of the model. MAS alignments of Matcha's own output are circular, whereas Parakeet-v3's word and character timestamps work for both languages.

Under deterministic, sentence-level rendering, M5 passes trivially, so M4 becomes the decisive cross-text test. M5 starts to matter once you add paragraph-context conditioning for richer prosody.

M3 also diagnoses your ElevenLabs voice today. Render the same paragraph with and without `next_text`. A rate difference shows that the slowdown depends on where the sentence falls in the request.

### Until your model ships, an ElevenLabs playbook

The documented levers work best applied in order. Set style to 0 and stability to moderate-to-high, fix the seed, and keep requests under 800 characters. Always pass `previous_text` and `next_text` ([API reference](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/api-reference/text-to-speech/convert)), or the IDs of neighbouring requests, which the stitching guide requires to be "no older than two hours" ([request stitching](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-api/guides/how-to/text-to-speech/request-stitching)). Stitching is unavailable on `eleven_v3`, and the PVC guide lists no v3 training. Then overshoot and trim: append the next sentence to every request and cut the audio at the aligned boundary, so the sentences you keep are never final. Measure each chunk's articulation rate, and re-render slow chunks with `speed` scaled toward the target within the permitted 0.7–1.2, or keep the closest of N takes ([speed](https://help.elevenlabs.io/hc/en-us/articles/13416271012497-Can-I-change-the-pace-of-the-voice)). Because `speed` is one scalar per request, it can re-center a slow take but cannot remove a slowdown inside one generation. The durable fix is retraining the PVC on 2–3 h of long, continuous, single-style narration at a consistent tempo. Switching models offers no documented advantage, since ElevenLabs already describes Multilingual v2 as "Most stable on long-form generations" ([models](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/models)).

## Seven plan defects would cause the failures the design prevents

Reviewing the implementation plan's code against this research turned up seven defects that would produce the very failures the architecture was chosen to eliminate. An independent verification pass confirmed all seven and sharpened several. It ran the plan's phonemizer code against espeak-ng 1.52 and checked the flow-matching direction on a toy model. Confidence is high.

The test suite is not purely shape-based. The text-normalization tests assert exact strings, and the alignment tests check that alignments are binary, monotonic and complete. But no test exercises any of the seven defects, and a test asserting 80 mel bands locks in defect 6. Run verbatim against espeak-ng, 13 of the 14 phonemizer tests pass despite the `<unk>` rates below. Two tests fail regardless of these defects. `test_mel_shape_calculation` fails because `center=True` framing yields 94 frames where the plan's formula expects 90. `test_soft_consonants` fails because espeak-ng 1.52 renders "päť" as `p ˈe tʲ`.

| # | Defect | What it causes | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The length regulator rounds durations with `torch.round`, clamps at 0, and keeps only tokens with `dur > 0` | `torch.round` rounds half to even. Any token predicted at 0.5 frames or less, or at a negative value, therefore gets zero frames: a skipped phoneme, and a run of them is a skipped word. In a mirror of the code, three of seven test durations (0.49, 0.5 and −0.3 frames) became zero | The root cause is `round()` where Matcha uses `ceil()`. Matcha computes `ceil(exp(logw))` and then multiplies by `length_scale` ([matcha_tts.py](https://github.com/shivammehta25/Matcha-TTS/blob/main/matcha/models/matcha_tts.py)), so its one-frame floor holds only at the default scale of 1. Apply any length scale first, then round up and clamp every non-padding token to at least one frame; StyleTTS2's demo uses `clamp(min=1)` ([notebook](https://github.com/yl4579/StyleTTS2/blob/main/Demo/Inference_LibriTTS.ipynb)). Never pass `target_len` at inference |
| 2 | The dataset crops the mel at `max_audio_length` and the phonemes at `max_text_length`, independently. This is latent: both limits default to `None`, and nothing in the plan sets them | Once either limit is set, any clip over it becomes a misaligned pair, in either direction: audio missing words the text contains, or audio for words the text no longer contains. MAS then bakes the mismatch into the alignment. This teaches the model either to skip and cut off, or to stretch phonemes over speech they do not match | Drop over-length utterances; never truncate one side |
| 3 | The flow-matching sampler runs opposite to training. Training puts data at t=0 and noise at t=1, with target `x1 − x0`; the sampler starts from noise at t=0 and adds the predicted velocity. The sampler matches Matcha's Euler solver; it is the training convention that swapped the endpoints | The sampler runs the data-to-noise flow, so it cannot produce a mel. In a toy test with the exact optimal field for data drawn from N(−5, 2²), the plan's sampler turned noise into N(+2.5, 0.5²), while a corrected sampler recovered N(−5, 2²). `sigma_min` is unused, which is harmless | Either integrate from t=1 to t=0 with x ← x − v·dt, or adopt Matcha's convention: noise at t=0, data at t=1, target `x1 − (1−σ_min)·x0`. Add a test that overfits one utterance and checks the reconstruction |
| 4 | The phoneme vocabulary is a fixed, language-independent hand list, with a silent `<unk>` fallback | Run against espeak-ng 1.52, the plan's code maps 41–62% of Czech and Slovak tokens and 88% of English tokens to `<unk>`, so the audio garbles. The causes: stress marks glued to vowels (`ˈo`); word boundaries and punctuation glued to phones (`iː\|`, `e?`); IPA `ɡ` where the list has ASCII `g`; unlisted symbols such as `r̝̊` and Slovak `dʲ`/`tʲ`; and missing English phones (ɪ, θ, ð, ɹ, ə). The default `create_phoneme_vocabulary()` call crashes, because espeak-ng rejects the language code "en" | Build the vocabulary from actual espeak-ng output over the whole corpus in all three languages. Tokenize stress, boundaries and punctuation separately, use a valid English code such as `en-us`, and raise an error on unknown symbols |
| 5 | Pronunciation overrides are a stub: `_apply_custom_pronunciation` returns its input unchanged | Lexicon fixes for foreign words and espeak-ng bugs silently do nothing | Apply word-level overrides before phonemization |
| 6 | Mel features do not match the vocoder. The plan computes 80 bands with torchaudio defaults: an HTK-scale, unnormalized filterbank with `center=True` framing. A test asserts 80 bands | BigVGAN v2's 24 kHz checkpoint expects 100 bands from a Slaney-scale, Slaney-normalized filterbank, with `(n_fft − hop)/2` reflect padding and `center=False` ([config](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/bigvgan_v2_24khz_100band_256x/raw/main/config.json); [BigVGAN](https://github.com/NVIDIA/BigVGAN)). The filterbank difference alone shifts log-mel values by a band-dependent 3–6 nats. The framing gives 93 frames instead of 94 for a 1 s clip, offset by half a hop. The 12 kHz fmax already matches, since BigVGAN's `null` resolves to Nyquist | Extract training mels with BigVGAN's own mel code at 100 bands, and update the test |
| 7 | Edge silence and level vary by session. Trimming cuts at −40 dB below the loudest frame's RMS, on 512-sample (about 21 ms) frame boundaries, with no pads. The "LUFS" step is peak normalization to 0.95, and `target_lufs` is never used | The amount of breath and room tone kept at clip edges varies with session loudness, biasing pause and rate learning. Soft onsets and word-final consonants can be cut, which teaches cut-off endings; in the verification run, a 200 ms onset at −60 dB was removed. Levels drift across sessions | Trim from forced alignment with fixed 100–200 ms pads, and normalize true loudness, for example with pyloudnorm, which the plan's requirements omit |

## Conclusion

The year is best read as the field converging on what your design already has. Autoregressive systems spent 2026 bolting duration and alignment back on, through token budgets, pause markers, rate-control states, monotonic attention priors, and rollback with temporary hard alignment masks. That makes the remaining risk in a Matcha-style system peripheral rather than architectural. It sits in text normalization, G2P, data segmentation, the length regulator and the verification loop, and engineering effort should follow it there. The pace requirement also reorders the upgrade path. RL, the year's most powerful new tool, can quietly break pace if its rewards ignore rate, so rate conditioning and pace metrics must exist before any duration RL begins.

The binding constraint is now measurement rather than modelling. No MOS predictor is validated for Czech or Slovak, and even TTSDS2's multilingual benchmark skips both languages. The best open ASR still errs on 5.5–11% of human speech, and no arena covers either language. A Slovak narrator model built this way will be ahead of published Slovak TTS research, so it has to produce its own ground truth: a few hundred native-listener judgments to calibrate automatic metrics, ASR thresholds set against the narrator's own recordings, and pace statistics from held-out narration. The order of work follows from that. Fix the seven defects, then audit and rate-label the narrator data. Train a rate-conditioned baseline behind ASR and pace gates, and add multilingual pretraining and the robustness objectives. Run the SSL-discriminator ablation after that, and apply RL last.
